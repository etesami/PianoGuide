# Piano Fundamentals

### Reading notes and finding your hand position

### A beginner's guide with practice drills for PianoGuide

## How to use this guide

You can already play with each hand, play a few notes with both hands, cross over, and stretch to a nearby key.
What slows you down is the step **before** playing: working out which key a note is, and where the hand should go.
This guide is about making that step quick, until it happens without stopping.

Three ideas carry the whole guide:

1. **Landmarks.** Learn a few notes so well that you see them instantly, on the staff and on the keyboard. Read every other note from the nearest landmark.
2. **Distances, not names.** Most of the time you don't need the name of the next note, only how far it is: a step, a skip, or more. On the keyboard and under your fingers, the distance is the same.
3. **Positions.** Put the hand over five keys *before* a phrase starts. After that, each note simply has a finger. You only think about the hand again when the music leaves those five keys.

Each chapter ends with something to do at the piano. Chapter 10 has eight practice drills. They are also in the
app, in the **Reading Guide** folder of the Songs list, so you can play them with the real piano and see each
note light up.

**A good pace:** one chapter and one or two drills per week. Short daily practice (15 to 20 minutes) works far
better than one long session.

## 1. The keyboard map

### The black keys are your guide

The black keys come in groups of **two** and **three**. That pattern repeats along the whole keyboard, and it
tells you where every white key is, without counting from the end.

<img src=figures/kb_groups.svg style=width:72%>

- **C** is the white key just **left of the two black keys**.
- **F** is the white key just **left of the three black keys**.
- From C, the white keys go up **C D E F G A B**, then C again. There is no H, and after G the alphabet starts again at A.
- **E** and **B** have no black key on their right. **C** and **F** have no black key on their left.

### Octaves and octave numbers

From one C to the next C is an **octave** (8 white keys). The octaves are numbered, and every note gets its
octave's number: the C in the middle of the keyboard is **C4**, called **Middle C**. The D just above it is D4,
the B just below it is B3. The number changes at every C.

<img src=figures/kb_octaves.svg style=width:100%>

The app and this guide use these names (C3, G4, ...). On an 88-key piano like the FP-30X, Middle C is the **fourth C from
the left**, a little left of the centre of the keyboard. Sit facing it.

### Black keys: sharps and flats

A black key is named after its white neighbour. Going **up** (right) from F, the black key is **F sharp**
(F♯). Going **down** (left) from B, the same kind of key is **B flat** (B♭). One black key has two names: the one
between A and B is both A♯ and B♭. For now you'll only meet two: **F♯** and **B♭**.

### At the piano

- Play every C on the keyboard, from the lowest to the highest. Then every F. Then every G.
- Close your eyes, find a group of two black keys by touch, and play the C below it.
- Play Middle C. Then find C3 and C5 (one octave down and up) without counting white keys: look for the two black keys.

## 2. Finger numbers

<img src=figures/hands.svg style=width:62%>

Both thumbs are **1**, and the little fingers are **5**. Because the hands are mirror images, the numbers run the
other way on the keyboard:

- **Right hand:** going **up** (to the right) is 1 → 5.
- **Left hand:** going **up** is 5 → 1. The left thumb is the hand's **highest** note.

That second point is the usual reason left-hand finger numbers feel backwards. Think from the thumbs: with the
palms down, both thumbs point toward the middle of the keyboard. So the right thumb plays the right hand's
**lowest** note, and the left thumb plays the left hand's **highest** note.

### At the piano

Rest the hands on the keys (right thumb on Middle C, left little finger on C3). Say a number and wiggle that finger in
both hands at once. Then tap 1-3-5, 2-4, 5-4-3-2-1 without looking.

## 3. The grand staff

Piano music is written on two staves joined by a brace: the **treble staff** (with the treble clef, usually the right hand) on top,
and the **bass staff** (with the bass clef, usually the left hand) below. Notes go up the staff in the same order as the white keys:
**each line and each space is the next white key.**

<img src=figures/treble_ladder.svg style=width:100%>

<img src=figures/bass_ladder.svg style=width:100%>

Notice the pattern: line, space, line, space. Moving from a line to the next space (or a space to the next line) is
always **one white key**.

### Lines and spaces

The classic memory sentences give you the lines and spaces of each staff. They help at the start, but don't lean
on them for long: reading from landmarks (chapter 4) is much faster.

<img src=figures/treble_lines_spaces.svg style=width:96%>

| Treble staff | Notes | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| Lines (bottom up) | E G B D F | **E**very **G**ood **B**oy **D**oes **F**ine |
| Spaces (bottom up) | F A C E | spells **FACE** |

<img src=figures/bass_lines_spaces.svg style=width:96%>

| Bass staff | Notes | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| Lines (bottom up) | G B D F A | **G**ood **B**oys **D**o **F**ine **A**lways |
| Spaces (bottom up) | A C E G | **A**ll **C**ows **E**at **G**rass |

### Middle C joins the two staves

Middle C sits on a short extra line (a **ledger line**) **below** the treble staff, or on one **above** the bass
staff. It is the same key either way. The notes around it can be written in either staff, which is why the two
staves feel like one continuous ladder.

<img src=figures/middle_c.svg style=width:96%>

Ledger lines continue the pattern of the staff: line, space, line. Count them from the staff, and the note is as
many steps beyond the outer line.

## 4. Landmark notes

Reading every note by counting lines from the bottom is what makes you stop. Instead, learn a few **landmarks**
until you recognise them instantly, as instantly as a word. Every other note is then only one or two steps away
from a landmark.

<img src=figures/landmarks_staff.svg style=width:100%>

<img src=figures/landmarks_keys.svg style=width:100%>

| Landmark | Where on the staff | On the keyboard |
|---|---|---|
| Middle C (C4) | 1 ledger line below the treble, or above the bass | the C in the middle |
| Treble G (G4) | 2nd line of the treble; the treble clef curls around it | 5 white keys above Middle C |
| Treble C (C5) | 3rd space of the treble | one octave above Middle C |
| High C (C6) | 2 ledger lines above the treble | two octaves above Middle C |
| Bass F (F3) | 4th line of the bass, between the clef's two dots | 5 white keys below Middle C |
| Bass C (C3) | 2nd space of the bass | one octave below Middle C |
| Bass G (G2) | bottom line of the bass | 4 white keys below Bass C |
| Low C (C2) | 2 ledger lines below the bass | two octaves below Middle C |

**Pattern to notice:** the C landmarks are spread evenly: Low C, Bass C, Middle C, Treble C, High C, one octave
apart. G and F sit right where the clefs point (the treble clef is also called the G clef, the bass clef the F clef).

### How to read a note from a landmark

1. Find the nearest landmark (on the staff it is usually at most two lines or spaces away).
2. Count the steps: each line or space is one white key.
3. Play that key by moving the same number of white keys from the landmark key.

Example: a note on the space just above Treble G is one step up from G: A4.

### At the piano

- Play each landmark while saying its name, low to high. Then high to low.
- Flashcards: write the eight landmarks on cards (staff on the front, name on the back). Two minutes a day until they are instant.
- Then play **drill 1** (right hand) and **drill 2** (left hand).

## 5. Reading by distance: steps and skips

The fastest readers don't name every note. They see the **shape**: up or down, and how far. A distance on the
staff is the same distance on the keyboard, and in a fixed position it is also the same distance between
fingers.

<img src=figures/intervals_staff.svg style=width:92%>

<img src=figures/intervals_keys.svg style=width:58%>

| On the staff | Name | Keyboard | Fingers (in a position) |
|---|---|---|---|
| line to next space (or space to line) | step (2nd) | next white key | next finger |
| line to next line (or space to space) | skip (3rd) | skip one white key | skip one finger |
| line to the 2nd space above | 4th | skip two keys | skip two fingers |
| line to the 2nd line above | 5th | skip three keys | finger 1 to finger 5 |

Repeated notes stay on the same line or space: same key, same finger.

### A way of looking

Before you play a bar, look at it for a moment and say the shape: "step up, step up, skip down". Your hand already
knows how to do a step or a skip, so once you've seen the shape, the fingers follow. You only need the
**name** of the first note.

### At the piano

Play **drill 3** (right hand) and **drill 4** (left hand). Each has a finger number only on the first note, on
purpose. Say the shapes out loud the first time.

## 6. Hand positions

A **position** is five white keys under the five fingers. Once the hand is in place, every note in those five
keys already has a finger, and you don't read the finger numbers any more: you read the note, and the finger is
already above the key.

Positions are named after a note. Here is **C position**: right thumb on Middle C, left little finger on Bass C.

<img src=figures/pos_c.svg style=width:96%>

| Note | C | D | E | F | G |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Right hand finger (C4 to G4) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| Left hand finger (C3 to G3) | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |

### Middle C position

Both thumbs share Middle C. The left hand covers F3 to C4, the right hand C4 to G4. Many beginner pieces use it,
because it keeps both hands near the middle of both staves.

<img src=figures/pos_middle_c.svg style=width:96%>

<img src=figures/middle_c_staff.svg style=width:90%>

### G position

Right thumb on Treble G, left little finger on Bass G (the bottom line of the bass staff). Same shape, a fifth higher.

<img src=figures/pos_g.svg style=width:100%>

### F position

Thumb on F. F position uses the black key **B♭** instead of B, so finger 4 of the right hand (and finger 2 of
the left) sits on a black key. In the drills here those fingers aren't used yet.

<img src=figures/pos_f.svg style=width:100%>

### Finding the position from the music

This is the most useful habit in this guide. Before a phrase, ask three questions:

1. **What is the first note?** Name it from a landmark.
2. **What are the lowest and highest notes of the phrase?** Scan ahead to the next rest or long note.
3. **Which five keys cover them?** Put the hand there. Now you know the finger for the first note.

<img src=figures/fit_phrase.svg style=width:82%>

The first note is E. The lowest is C (Middle C) and the highest is G: five keys, C to G. That is C position, so the
right thumb goes on C, and the first note, E, is finger **3**.

<img src=figures/fit_keys.svg style=width:56%>

Every finger in the phrase now follows from the position:

<img src=figures/fit_answer.svg style=width:82%>

**You never had to read a finger number.** If the music does give you one (like the 3 on the first note),
use it to check: finger 3 on E means the thumb is on C.

### At the piano

- Put both hands in C position, Middle C position, G position and F position, one after another, without
  looking at the keyboard for more than a moment. Use the black key groups to find them.
- Take any phrase from a song you know and answer the three questions before playing it.

## 7. Moving the hand

Music rarely stays in five keys for long. There are four ways to reach outside a position. Knowing which one
the music needs takes most of the guessing out of it.

### 1. Moving the whole position (a shift)

The hand keeps its shape and **lifts, moves and lands** on the new five keys. The moment to move is during a
**long note or a rest**. While you hold the note, look ahead, find the new first note (from a landmark), and
place the finger that goes on it.

<img src=figures/shift_staff.svg style=width:82%>

<img src=figures/shift_keys.svg style=width:62%>

Here the whole note C in bar 3 is the time to move: while it sounds, look at bar 4, see Treble G with a 1, and
let the thumb land on G. The rest of bar 4 is just G position.

**Rules for a calm shift:**

- Move the **whole hand** as one shape. Don't stretch toward the new key and drag the hand after it.
- **Aim for one key** only: the first note of the new position, with its finger. The other fingers land by themselves.
- Look ahead **one bar**: the move is decided before you get there.
- If there is no long note, the composer usually leaves a short rest or a repeated note. Play slowly at first, and the move will fit.

### 2. Thumb under (going up in the right hand, down in the left)

For runs longer than five notes, the hand doesn't jump. After finger 3, the thumb passes **under** the hand to
the next key, and the hand follows.

<img src=figures/thumb_under.svg style=width:62%>

### 3. Cross-over (going down in the right hand, up in the left)

The opposite direction: finger 3 (sometimes 4) crosses **over** the thumb.

<img src=figures/cross_over.svg style=width:62%>

### 4. Extension (a stretch)

When only one note is outside the position, keep the hand where it is and **reach** with the nearest finger.
Then come back to the position.

<img src=figures/extension.svg style=width:62%>

### Which one?

| The music... | Use |
|---|---|
| leaves the five keys for a whole phrase, after a long note or rest | a shift: move the position |
| runs on in one direction, step by step, past five keys | thumb under / cross-over |
| touches one note just outside the five keys, then comes back | an extension |
| jumps far, to a different area (e.g. to Middle C from High C) | a shift to a landmark |

### At the piano

Play **drill 5** (right hand moves between C and G), **drill 6** (both hands move between C, G and F) and
**drill 7** (thumb under and cross-over in the C major scale).

## 8. Reading without finger numbers

In real music, finger numbers are rare: maybe one at the start and one where the hand has to move. This is how to
play without them, step by step. Use it at the start of every phrase.

**Find, Fit, Flow**

1. **Find.** Name the first note from the nearest landmark. Find the key using the black key groups.
2. **Fit.** Look at the whole phrase (up to the next rest or long note): lowest and highest notes. Choose the five keys that cover them, place the hand, and put the right finger on the first note.
3. **Flow.** Play, reading **distances**: step = next finger, skip = skip a finger, same note = same finger. Look one bar ahead. When a note falls outside the hand, decide: extension, shift, or thumb under (chapter 7).

**When a finger number is printed,** use it for **Fit**: "3 on E" tells you the whole position (thumb on C).
Printed finger numbers usually appear exactly where the hand moves, so they are hints about **where the position
changes**, not instructions for every note.

### A pencil habit

When you learn a new piece, mark only the **position changes**: write the position's name (C, G, F) or the finger
for the first note after each move. Leave everything else unmarked. You'll read faster than with every finger
written in.

### Using the app

- Open **Songs → Reading Guide**. Start in **Wait** mode: the music waits for each correct note, so you can take
  time to think. Tap **Listen** (in Timed mode) to hear how it should sound.
- When a drill feels easy, hide the finger numbers with the **123** button and play it again.
- Then switch to **Timed** mode at a slow speed (50 to 60%), and raise it a little each day.

## 9. Rhythm in one page

<img src=figures/rhythm.svg style=width:100%>

| Note | Looks like | Beats (in 4/4) |
|---|---|---|
| whole note | hollow head, no stem | 4 |
| half note | hollow head, with stem | 2 |
| quarter note | filled head, with stem | 1 |
| eighth note | filled head, stem and flag | ½ |

Rests are silent beats of the same lengths: whole rest (hangs from a line), half rest (sits on a line), quarter
rest (a zigzag).

<img src=figures/rests.svg style=width:72%>

**4/4** at the start means 4 quarter-note beats in every bar. Count "1 2 3 4" out loud, steadily, while you play:
it keeps you moving through the moments you would otherwise stop. A slow, steady count is better than a fast one
with pauses.

## 11. A practice plan

### Every day (15 to 20 minutes)

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 2 min | Find every C, F and G; find them with eyes closed | the keyboard map |
| 3 min | Landmark flashcards, then their neighbours | instant reading |
| 10 min | Today's drill: Wait mode, then fingers off, then Timed | reading while playing |
| 3 min | A song you like, using Find, Fit, Flow | using it for real |

### Four weeks

| Week | Read | Drills | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | chapters 1 to 4 | 1, 2 | name all landmarks instantly |
| 2 | chapter 5 | 3, 4 | play by distance, no finger numbers |
| 3 | chapters 6, 7 | 5, 6 | move the hand without stopping |
| 4 | chapters 8, 9 | 7, 8 | Find, Fit, Flow on a new piece |

Repeat a week if it doesn't feel easy yet: that is normal. Speed comes from many slow, calm repetitions, not from
trying to play fast.

### When something goes wrong

| Problem | Try this |
|---|---|
| I stop to work out each note's name | Only name the first note of a phrase; read the rest as steps and skips. Practise flashcards for landmarks. |
| I don't know where to put the hand | Find, Fit, Flow: lowest and highest notes of the phrase decide the position. |
| Moving the hand makes me stop | Move during the long note before; look one bar ahead; aim for one key only. |
| Left-hand fingers feel backwards | Remember: the left thumb is the **highest** note of the left hand. |
| I look down at my hands all the time | Find the position once by looking, then keep the eyes on the music; feel the black keys. |
| Too many wrong notes in Timed mode | Lower the speed, or go back to Wait mode for a few days. |
